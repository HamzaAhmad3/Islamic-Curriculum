Ali ibn Abd al-Kafi al-Subki, known as Taqi al-Din, was born in Safar 683 AH in Subk, a village in Egypt's Manufiyyah province. He studied fiqh first under his father, then in Cairo under Ibn al-Rif'ah, took usul al-din and kalam from Ali al-Baji, khilaf and mantiq from Sayf al-Baghdadi, nahw from the celebrated grammarian Abu Hayyan al-Andalusi, tafsir from 'Ilm al-Din al-'Iraqi, qira'at from al-Taqi al-Sa'igh, hadith from al-Sharaf al-Dimyati, and tasawwuf from Taj al-Din ibn Ata'illah. He traveled widely in pursuit of hadith, reaching Bilad al-Sham, Alexandria, and the Hejaz.

He was appointed Qadi al-Qudat (Chief Judge) of Damascus in Jumada al-Akhirah 739 AH, succeeding Jalal al-Din al-Qazwini, and combined the post with the khataba (preaching) of the Umayyad Mosque. After the death of al-Mizzi, he was given the mashyakha of Dar al-Hadith al-Ashrafiyyah — a post whose endowment conditions required an Ash'ari-creed shaykh, which told against the otherwise leading candidate, al-Dhahabi, whose theological positions were then disputed. Contemporaries described him as combining exceptional command of hadith, tafsir, fiqh, usul, nahw, and kalam with real personal austerity — al-Dhahabi, who studied under him, called him unmatched in his generation for both learning and character.

Al-Subki is also remembered for his willingness to publicly contest Ibn Taymiyyah on two specific rulings: the permissibility of traveling specifically to visit the Prophet's grave, which Ibn Taymiyyah had restricted, and whether a triple-divorce pronounced in a single utterance counts as one or three — writing dedicated treatises defending the mainstream position on each. His fatwas, compiled by his son Taj al-Din into four volumes, and his major fiqh commentary **al-Ibtihaj fi Sharh al-Minhaj**, remained influential long after his death in Jumada al-Akhirah 756 AH.
