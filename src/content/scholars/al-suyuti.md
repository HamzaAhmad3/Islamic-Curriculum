Abd al-Rahman ibn Abi Bakr, Jalal al-Din al-Suyuti, was born in Cairo in Rajab 849 AH and orphaned young — his father died when he was about five or six. He memorized the Qur'an before he was eight, then 'Umdat al-Ahkam, al-Minhaj (fiqh and usul), and Alfiyyat Ibn Malik, beginning serious study in 864 AH. By his own account he attained mastery (tabahhur) in seven sciences — tafsir, hadith, fiqh, nahw, ma'ani, bayan, and badi' — and studied further in usul al-fiqh, jadal, sarf, inshaʼ, fara'id, and qira'at. He began compiling books in 866 AH and, by his own later count, had written some 300 works; other sources, including Ibn Iyas, put his lifetime output closer to 600. He traveled to Bilad al-Sham, the Hejaz, Yemen, India, and the Maghrib, and began issuing fatwas in 871 AH.

His scholarly range was genuinely encyclopedic: **al-Itqan fi 'Ulum al-Qur'an** and **al-Durr al-Manthur** remain standard references in Qur'anic sciences and exegesis; **Tafsir al-Jalalayn**, begun by his teacher Jalal al-Din al-Mahalli and completed by al-Suyuti after al-Mahalli's death, is still one of the most widely read tafsirs in the Muslim world; and works like **Tadrib al-Rawi**, **Bughyat al-Wu'ah**, and **Husn al-Muhadarah** remain cited references in hadith methodology, the history of grammarians, and the history of Cairo respectively.

Al-Suyuti's later life was marked by a well-documented rivalry with his contemporary al-Sakhawi, who wrote a harshly critical entry on him in al-Daw' al-Lami', accusing him of exaggerating his own learning and repackaging earlier scholars' work; al-Suyuti responded in kind with a treatise titled al-Kawi fi al-Radd 'ala al-Sakhawi. Later biographers, including al-Shawkani in al-Badr al-Tali', pushed back on al-Sakhawi's account as reflecting personal rivalry rather than fair judgment — noting that hadith-critical principles themselves caution against accepting one peer's harsh assessment of another where open rivalry is documented. After the age of forty, al-Suyuti largely withdrew from public life to Rawdat al-Miqyas on the Nile, declining gifts and repeated invitations from sultans and amirs, and continued writing in seclusion until his death on 9 Jumada al-Ula 911 AH.
